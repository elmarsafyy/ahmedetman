TITLE:
What If You Opened the ISS Door in Space? 😱🚀 #Shorts

DESCRIPTION:
What would actually happen if you opened the door of the International Space Station and stepped outside? Spoiler: it's not like the movies. 🚀

🚪 Good news first: you can't just open it. The ISS hatches open INWARD, and the air inside pushes on them with almost 15 pounds on every square inch (14.7 psi, the same as sea level on Earth). On a hatch that size, that adds up to more than 35,000 pounds. That's roughly three elephants leaning on the door to keep it shut.

🌀 But say you're inside the airlock and the outer hatch fails. WHOOSH. The air rushes out, and anything that isn't tied down goes with it. The temperature drops so fast that the cabin fills with fog. Then the sound fades away... because sound needs air to travel. Total silence.

🫁 The single most important thing you can do? BREATHE OUT. If you hold your breath, the air in your lungs expands and can rupture them. Divers learn the same rule.

💧 You don't explode, and your blood doesn't boil (your skin and blood vessels hold it under pressure). But the water on your tongue does. In 1966, NASA engineer Jim LeBlanc was accidentally exposed to near-vacuum during a spacesuit test in a vacuum chamber in Houston. The last thing he remembered before blacking out? The saliva bubbling on his tongue. He survived.

⏱️ Your skin and tissue start to swell, and you have about 15 seconds of useful consciousness before you pass out.

🌡️ You also won't freeze instantly. Space is basically a giant thermos: with no air, heat leaves your body slowly. But in direct sunlight, surfaces can hit around +250°F, and in the shade they can drop to around -250°F.

🆘 If someone gets you back into air within about a minute and a half, you have a real chance of surviving.

🍃 And here's the wild part: the station really does spring leaks. In 2020, the crew spent weeks hunting a tiny air leak in the Russian Zvezda module. How did they finally pinpoint it? By releasing floating tea leaves and watching where they drifted. 🫖

So yeah... maybe keep that door closed. 😅

⚡ Quick facts
• ISS cabin pressure: about 14.7 psi, same as sea level.
• Force on a closed hatch: over 35,000 lb.
• Useful consciousness in vacuum: about 15 seconds.
• Survival window if repressurized: about 90 seconds.
• Space surface temps: about +250°F in sunlight, -250°F in shade.
• 1966: NASA's Jim LeBlanc survived near-vacuum in a test chamber.
• 2020: an ISS leak was tracked down with floating tea leaves.

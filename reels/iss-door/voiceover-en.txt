What if you opened the door of the International Space Station... and stepped outside?

Good news first. You can't. The hatches open inward, and the air inside pushes on them with almost fifteen pounds on every square inch. On a hatch this size, that's over thirty-five thousand pounds. Almost three elephants, holding the door shut.

But let's say you're in the airlock... and the outer hatch fails.

Whoosh. The air rushes out. Anything not tied down goes with it. The temperature drops so fast, the cabin fills with fog. And then... the sound just fades away. Silence.

Now, the most important thing you can do? Breathe out. Hold your breath, and your lungs can rupture.

You don't explode. Your blood doesn't boil. But the water on your tongue does. In nineteen sixty-six, a NASA engineer was exposed to near-vacuum in a test chamber. The last thing he remembered? His saliva bubbling on his tongue.

Your skin swells. And you have about fifteen seconds before you pass out.

You won't freeze instantly, either. Space is like a giant thermos. In the sun, surfaces hit two hundred fifty degrees. In the shade, minus two hundred fifty.

If someone gets air back to you within about a minute and a half, you could survive.

And here's the wild part. The station does spring leaks. In twenty twenty, the crew tracked one down... using floating tea leaves.

So yeah. Maybe keep that door closed.
